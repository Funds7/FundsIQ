GST 101: Chapter 1 — An Introduction to English Lexis and Structure

  1. Lexis and structure are also known as the study of the word and the structure of the sentence constitute the two most basic units of grammar.
  2. For Huddleston, the two most basic units of grammar are the word and the sentence.
  3. The two most basic units of grammar are the word and the sentence.
  4. Morphology deals with forms of words.
  5. Syntax deals with the way words combine to form sentences.
  6. Vocabulary refers to words and their precise dictionary meanings.
  7. Lexis refers to words and their various connections and allocation such as their synonyms, antonyms, homonyms, idioms and figurative usages.
  8. Word can be divided and interpreted in three ways namely spoken and written word, word forms and lexeme, and lexical and grammatical words.
  9. The spoken form of words involves pronunciation while the written form is the orthographic version or spelling of a word.
  10. Homophones are words pronounced the same way but have different spellings and meanings e.g feet/feat and lesson/lessen.
  11. Homonyms involve a case where a single spelling and pronunciation occur with more than one meaning e.g bank.
  12. For Jackson, homophone, homograph and homonym are derived from Greek.
  13. Homograph means the same letter.
  14. Homophone means the same sound.
  15. Homonym means the same name.
  16. The lexeme is the headword or dictionary entry of a word.
  17. The words boy, boys and boy's have the lexeme boy as the base form.
  18. Words are studied under a branch of linguistics called morphology.
  19. Word is made up of three principal parts namely prefix, root and suffix.
  20. The root of any word is also known as the free morpheme.
  21. The root or free morpheme can stand alone and function as a word.
  22. The part before the root is called prefix while the one after the root is suffix.
  23. Examples of only root words are the, not and educate.
  24. Examples of prefix plus base are intake and re-educate.
  25. Ebele defined register as vocabulary associated with various fields of human activity.
  26. Register of building includes concrete, pillar, foreman, bricklayer and scaffolding.
  27. Register of the bank includes crossed cheque, open cheque and bounced cheque.
  28. Synonyms are words that have almost the same meaning.
  29. Examples of synonyms are battle and conflict.
  30. Antonyms are pairs of words opposite in meaning.
  31. For Ejiogu, an idiom is a combination of words whose meaning differs from the meanings of the individual words.
  32. A combination of words in English is known as collocation.
  33. Examples of idioms include take the bull by the horn, rock the boat and rain cats and dogs.
  34. A clause consists of a subject noun phrase and a verb phrase.
  35. Examples of finite clauses include Who came here yesterday? and The boy won the prize.
  36. The types of clause include main clause and subordinate clause.
  37. A main clause can stand alone e.g I jumped over the fence.
  38. A subordinate clause cannot stand on its own.
  39. The three main types of finite subordinate clauses are relative, noun and adverbial clauses.
  40. Examples of relative clauses include Who came yesterday and That is my boy.
  41. An example of a noun clause is That John travelled yesterday.
  42. Examples of adverbial clauses include If it rains and Because I was sick.
  43. A basic English clause or sentence is divided into subject and predicate.
  44. Noun phrases function mostly as the subject of a sentence.
  45. Verb phrases function as the verb or predicator of a clause.
  46. The complement of a sentence is divided into complement and object.
  47. Noun phrases and adjective phrases function mostly as complements.
  48. Adverb phrases and prepositional phrases function mostly as adjuncts.
  49. There are four types of clauses in English namely declarative, imperative, interrogative and exclamative.
  50. A declarative clause is known as the unmarked type.
  51. Examples include The man is old, Come here, Is the man tall? and How careful you are.
  52. A declarative clause always ends with a full stop.
  53. An interrogative clause is a clause that requires an answer.
  54. For Adejare, the sentence is the largest grammatical unit.
  55. When a main clause is alone it is known as a simple sentence.
  56. A compound sentence consists of two or more main clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction.
  57. Examples of coordinating conjunctions are and, but and or.
  58. A complex sentence consists of one or more main clauses and one or more subordinate clauses.

GST 101: Chapter 2 — Essay Writing, Paragraph and Punctuation

  1. An essay can be defined as any piece of writing on related ideas with each idea overlapping with one another until they form a unified whole.
  2. A text is any piece of writing of any length that has unity.
  3. The material required for essay writing is language.
  4. The major aim of the essay is to communicate.
  5. To write a good essay, the essayist must be in control of the idea which he wants to communicate to readers.
  6. The types of essays include narrative essay, descriptive essay, expository essay, and argumentative essay.
  7. The purpose of a narrative essay is to tell a story in chronological order.
  8. For effective narration, use the past tense because the events occurred in the past.
  9. The purpose of a descriptive essay is to evoke a mood or create a dominant impression about a person, object, or place.
  10. To write a good descriptive essay, the writer must be a good observer of events and objects.
  11. Effective description requires good language use, engaging the senses, and giving specific details.
  12. The purpose of an expository essay is to inform, clarify, explain or show how something is done.
  13. An argumentative essay is also called a persuasive essay and its purpose is to change the reader's point of view.
  14. A good argumentative essay should be objective, based on facts, reasons, and statistics.
  15. The stages of essay writing include pre-writing, writing, and re-writing stages.
  16. The pre-writing stage involves choosing a topic, gathering facts, analyzing ideas, and outlining.
  17. An outline is a graphical presentation of information and the relationship between ideas.
  18. The types of outline include topic outline, sentence outline, and paragraph outline.
  19. A good outline helps to organize ideas and improves the structure of an essay.
  20. The writing stage is the actual process of writing the essay.
  21. The re-writing stage involves revising, editing, and proofreading the essay.
  22. A good paragraph must have unity, completeness, order, and coherence.
  23. A paragraph should focus on one main idea only.
  24. A full stop marks the end of a sentence and indicates the longest pause.
  25. A question mark is used at the end of interrogative sentences.
  26. An exclamation mark is used to show strong emotion or surprise.
  27. A comma is the shortest pause in writing.
  28. A semi-colon is used to join two related independent clauses.
  29. A colon is used to introduce a list or explanation.
  30. A dash is used to show a sudden change of thought.
  31. Capital letters are used to begin sentences and proper nouns.
  32. A hyphen is used to join prefixes such as ex- and anti-.
  33. An apostrophe is used for contractions and possession.
  34. Quotation marks are used to enclose direct speech or quotations.
  35. An ellipsis is used to show omission of words.
  36. Brackets are used to add extra information or explanations in a sentence.

GST 101: Chapter 3 — Phonetics

  1. The term 'organ of speech' is used to refer to the various parts of the human anatomy.
  2. The organs of speech include lungs, the larynx, the pharynx, velum or soft palate, hard palate, nasal cavity, lips, and teeth.
  3. Roach defines vowels as sounds in which there is no obstruction to the flow of air as it passes from the larynx to the lips.
  4. Vowels in English are divided into two classes: pure and impure vowels.
  5. /i:/ is pronounced 'ee' and it is the vowel used in the word 'see'.
  6. /i/ is a front vowel.
  7. /e/ is a short vowel used in words like 'den' and 'ten' and it is a front vowel produced with spread lips.
  8. Diphthongs are produced with no obstruction to the free flow of air out of the mouth.
  9. A consonant is a sound produced when two or more organs come firmly, intermittently or partially together.
  10. Place of articulation refers to the point along the vocal tract where the sound is produced.
  11. The different places of articulation include bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, palato-alveolar, etc.
  12. Bilabial means the upper and the lower lips.
  13. Labiodental refers to the lip and teeth.
  14. Manner of articulation is concerned with how sounds are produced.
  15. Affricates are sounds produced with a complete closure followed by a slow release.
  16. Lateral are sounds made with the front of the tongue forming a complete closure but with air escaping over the sides of the tongue.
  17. Semi-vowels or approximants are sounds that share the characteristics of both vowels and consonants.
  18. The syllable can be defined as the minimum or shortest stretch of speech we can pronounce.
  19. Stress is the relatively greater energy or force used in the pronunciation of a syllable.
  20. Chukwu defines intonation as pitch variation superimposed on word-groups in connected speech.
  21. Intonation is combined with accent to determine the meaning of utterances.
  22. The types of intonation include falling tune, rising tune, rise-fall, and fall-rise.

GST 101: Chapter 4 — Reading and Studying Skills

  1. Anderson (2006) sees reading as the process of constructing meaning from written texts.
  2. Stebbins (2011) defines reading as a complex cognitive process of decoding symbols(words) to construct or derive meaning.
  3. Reading involves the mastery of interrelated functions which include: the skills and knowledge to understand how phonemes are connected to print, identification of the letters of the alphabet including a recognition of the differences in printed symbols.
  4. Medical factors that could be responsible for deficiency in reading include dyslexia and aphasia.
  5. Dyslexia is a reading disability linked to the brain characterized by difficulty in recognizing and getting meaning from printed text.
  6. Aphasia is a disorder caused by damage to the left part of the brain which controls the ability to acquire, learn, and use language.
  7. Reading problems associated with bad reading and studying habits include Vocalization, regression, word-by-word reading, pointing to words, recognition span, sub-vocalization, head movement, poor vocabulary, and inflexible reading.
  8. Readers who vocalize read with their mouths.
  9. Regression is rereading a word, phrase or sentence, not because it is necessary for comprehension, but out of habit.
  10. Bad readers tend to see words singly and in isolation.
  11. Pointing at words drastically reduces speed and limits comprehension and distorts the meaning relations that exist between words in a passage.
  12. Recognition span is the amount of written text that a reader is capable of perceiving at a glance.
  13. The kinds of reading include Skimming, scanning, proofreading, revision reading, extensive and intensive reading, critical reading, etc.
  14. Skimming is a method of rapidly moving the eyes over text to get only the main ideas and a general overview of the content.
  15. Scanning is used to rapidly cover a great deal of materials to locate a specific fact or piece of information.
  16. Proofreading involves carefully reading and rereading a document intended for publication or a thesis, it is done to eliminate spelling, punctuation, or grammatical errors.
  17. Revision reading is undertaken to confirm knowledge that is previously known, usually in a material that is familiar to the reader.
  18. Extensive reading is geared towards wide coverage of a text.
  19. Intensive reading occurs when the learner is focused on the language rather than the text.
  20. Intensive reading focuses on the relationship between words, phrases, sentences and how they combine to realize the message of the text.
  21. Critical reading is a form of reading that does not take the given text at face value. It involves a deeper examination of the claims made by the writer.
  22. Critical reading examines arguments and attempts to provide possible counterarguments.
  23. Some study methods include: SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, and Review), PQRST (Preview, Question, Read, Summary, and Test), 3S3R (Survey, Study-read, Speed-read, Recite, Review, and Reflect).
  24. The SQ3R method of reading was introduced by Francis Pleasant Robinson (1946).
  25. The PQRST method of reading places emphasis on acquiring information in a way that is directly connected to how students are expected to use the information in an examination scenario.
  26. The 3S3R study method was developed by Unoh.
  27. Evoker was developed by Walter Paul in 1962. It is designed for studying literature (prose, poetry, and drama).
  28. Evoker is made up of six stages namely: 1. Explore 2. Vocabulary 3. Oral Reading 4. Key Ideas 5. Evaluate 6. Recapitulation.
  29. The tips for effective study include: 1. Reading should be purpose-oriented 2. The essence of reading is to remember 3. Skip irrelevant materials 4. Read for ideas etc.

GST 101: Chapter 6 — Sentence Construction

  1. The verbal group or a verb phrase is headed by a verb, the word class used to express processes, events, actions, states, and relations within a clause e.g She 'cried', They 'left' here two hours ago.
  2. The adjectival group is headed by the word class adjective and it functions as a complement in the structure of a clause e.g. She is very beautiful, the food is too sweet, etc.
  3. The prepositional group is headed by a preposition, its function is to show the relationship between two entities e.g The books are on the table.
  4. The grammatical unit immediately above the group and immediately below the sentence is the clause.
  5. A clause contains four elements of clause structure in the order: Subject, predicator, complement, and adjunct abbreviated as S, P, C, A.
  6. Mary is fine this evening follows SPCA.
  7. They made Jonathan the President follows SPCC.
  8. Wash the rice following PC.
  9. They sang the chorus lustily follows SPCA.
  10. They made him their leader last year following SPCCA.
  11. The young shall grow follows SP.
  12. Jane is very beautiful follows SPC.
  13. The former president is Dr Goodluck Jonathan follows SPC.
  14. Clauses are classified into two types using the structural and functional criteria.
  15. A clause is said to be independent if it has a form in which it could stand as an independent sentence.
  16. A dependent clause (subordinate clause) cannot stand alone as an independent sentence, it needs an independent clause to be able to function.
  17. In terms of discourse functions, there are four forms of clauses namely declarative clause, interrogative clause, imperative clause, and exclamatory clause.
  18. A declarative clause states information or an opinion. It is also called a statement as it is used to state facts, ideas, and opinions and to inform. Ekpe is my grandmother's name.
  19. An interrogative clause is used to ask questions. e.g what is our name.
  20. An imperative clause is used to express a command, an instruction, a directive or a request e.g. Praise the Lord.
  21. An exclamatory clause is used to express shock, surprise, or to express how a speaker is emotionally impressed. What a nice meal she cooked.
  22. Sentences can be grouped into three namely: Simple, compound, and complex sentences.
  23. The simple sentence consists of an independent alpha clause which means that it can function on its own e.g Jack is a very ferocious dog.
  24. A compound sentence consists of two or more independent clauses linked by appropriate coordinators e.g. Take her to the police station or Set her free.
  25. The complex sentence requires the presence of two clauses as its primary constituent eg. Unless you pay your NEPA bill, your electricity will be disconnected.

  28. Fair dealings cover noncommercial use of works such as for the purpose of private research, criticism, review and reporting current events.

GST 102: Chapter 1 — General Introduction to Philosophy

  1. Western philosophy which originated from Greece began when the earliest Greek philosophers became dissatisfied with mythological explanation of reality.
  2. Etymologically, philosophy is derived from two Greek words, 'Philos' and 'Sophia' meaning 'love' and 'wisdom'.
  3. Philosophy is the love of wisdom.
  4. In the academic sense, philosophy is to critically search and probe to understand the reality of things in themselves and being in itself.
  5. All philosophy should lead to ethics if not it will be useless.
  6. Ethics is the endpoint and destination of philosophical knowledge.
  7. People's beliefs, opinions, positions, way of life, orientations, and worldview could be considered as philosophy.
  8. Philosophy is a rigorous academic discipline that involves using the critical tool of human reasoning to search into the issues of being, existence, and life in all its ramifications.
  9. You can be a philosopher without being located in the classroom.
  10. Philosophers are of various orientations depending on the branch of philosophy that they specialize in e.g philosopher of education, philosopher of religion, philosopher of science, etc.
  11. Some people conceive philosophy as occultism such as witchcraft, because of its branch metaphysics which deals with realities that are beyond the physical like being, God, etc.
  12. Some conceive philosophy as anti-religious and philosophers as atheists.
  13. Philosophy can be understood from two perspectives namely: as a way of life and as an academic discipline.
  14. Philosophy as a way of life explains how most people and organizations or institutions are said to have philosophies of life due to the very practical fact that they have developed.
  15. Philosophy is the science that investigates the highest cause of all things as far as they are knowable.
  16. The activity of critical and reflective thinking is central to philosophical activities.
  17. The spirit of philosophy is critical reasoning.
  18. The four branches of philosophy include: 1. Metaphysics 2. Epistemology 3. Logic 4. Ethics.
  19. The word metaphysics is derived from two Greek words 'meta' which means after or beyond, and 'physika' which means nature.
  20. Andronicus of Rhodes was the compiler of Aristotle's works; he came up with the name metaphysics.
  21. Metaphysics is the science that deals with being as being.
  22. Metaphysics studies the essence of being.
  23. Ontology and theodicy are branches of metaphysics.
  24. Ontology critically probes into the nature and essence of being/reality.
  25. Theodicy studies the problem of evil and the existence of a good God.
  26. Cosmology is a branch of metaphysics that is concerned with the origin, nature, and structure of the universe.

GST 102: Chapter 8 — Definition and Scope of Logic

  1. For philosophers to perform their work well, they are required to use correct (valid) arguments. They must be able to differentiate between valid and invalid arguments.
  2. The study of the methods or principles used by philosophers to test the validity or invalidity of arguments is known as logic.
  3. Logic may be defined as the art of argumentation or the science of reasoning.
  4. Types of logic include: (1) Traditional (formal) logic developed by Aristotle, and (2) Symbolic or mathematical logic.
  5. Traditional (formal) logic was first developed by Aristotle.
  6. Formal logic is concerned with the formal and general structure of thought.
  7. Formal logic is a priori in character; it deals with the form of thought and does not rely on empirical principles.
  8. Syllogisms are deductive arguments consisting of three categorical propositions.
  9. In syllogisms, two statements are premises while the third is the conclusion.
  10. An example of a syllogism is: All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore, Socrates is mortal.
  11. When considering the validity of an argument, what matters is its logical form rather than the content of its statements.
  12. The two main types of symbolic logic are logistics and metalogic.
  13. Logistics deals with the calculus of logic.
  14. Metalogic deals with the formal conditions of logical systems.
  15. Metalogic includes syntax and semantics.
  16. Notable figures in the development of symbolic logic include Peano, Tarski, Gödel, and Russell.
  17. Logic is concerned with reasoning validly or soundly.
  18. What differentiates humans from lower animals is the capacity to reason.
  19. For Frank Harrison, propositions used to establish the truth or falsity of a conclusion are called premises.
  20. An example of an argument is: All men are mortal; John is a man; therefore, John is mortal.
  21. An argument with two premises and a conclusion is known as a syllogism.
  22. Arguments consist of premises, conclusions, and inference.
  23. A proposition is the meaning or idea expressed in a sentence.
  24. A sentence is a combination of words arranged according to grammatical rules.
  25. A proposition is either true or false and is represented in symbolic logic using letters such as P, Q, R, and S.
  26. Premises are propositions that provide reasons for accepting the conclusion of an argument.
  27. Indicators of premises include words such as 'since', 'because', 'for', and 'inasmuch as'.
  28. Inferring is the process by which a conclusion is derived from one or more premises.
  29. The conclusion is the proposition affirmed based on the premises.
  30. Types of arguments include deductive and inductive arguments.
  31. In deductive arguments, premises provide necessary and conclusive grounds for the conclusion.
  32. In deductive logic, the terms 'valid' and 'invalid' are used instead of 'correct' and 'incorrect'.
  33. A deductive argument is valid only if its premises guarantee the truth of its conclusion.
  34. The premises of an inductive argument do not guarantee the truth of the conclusion.
  35. In strong inductive arguments, the premises provide high support for the conclusion, while in weak inductive arguments, they provide little support.
  36. Inductive reasoning moves from particular instances to general conclusions, for example: The goat seen today is black; the one seen yesterday is black; therefore, all goats are black.
  37. Unlike deductive arguments, inductive arguments can be strengthened or weakened by adding more premises, for example: Most Nigerian lawyers are civil rights activists; Mudiaga Odje is a Nigerian lawyer; therefore, Mudiaga Odje is probably a civil rights activist.
  38. The logician is concerned with the validity of arguments.
  39. An invalid argument occurs when the premises do not provide sufficient justification for the conclusion.
  40. An argument is invalid if its premises do not support its conclusion.
  41. The argument: If anyone is from Nigeria, then he is African; Shehu is a Nigerian; therefore, Shehu is an African, is valid and sound.
  42. Validity concerns logical form, while soundness concerns both form and truth of the content.
  43. An unsound deductive argument may be valid, but its premises or conclusion may be false.
  44. An example of a valid but unsound argument is: All mammals are cold-blooded; animals are mammals; therefore, some cold-blooded animals are reptiles.
  45. The three laws of thought are: the law of identity, the law of non-contradiction, and the law of excluded middle.
  46. The law of identity states that if a statement is true, then it is true; a thing is identical with itself.
  47. The law of non-contradiction states that a statement cannot be both true and false at the same time.
  48. The law of excluded middle states that every statement is either true or false.

GST 102: Chapter 9 — General Natures of Fallacies

  1. A fallacy can be defined simply as an error in reasoning.
  2. Fallacies are not meant to be persuasive, but they often are. They may be committed unintentionally or intentionally to deceive others.
  3. The first known study of fallacies was carried out by Aristotle in his work 'De Sophisticis Elenchis'.
  4. A formal fallacy can be detected by examining the logical form of an argument.
  5. An informal fallacy depends on the content and purpose of the reasoning.
  6. You commit the Ad Hominem (circumstantial) fallacy whenever you make an irrelevant attack on the arguer instead of addressing the argument.
  7. The accent fallacy is a fallacy of ambiguity caused by different ways a word or phrase is emphasized.
  8. An example of the accent fallacy is when a National Assembly member responds to a question about supporting a missile defense system by saying, 'I support a missile defense system that effectively defends Nigeria'.
  9. Anthropomorphism is the error of attributing human qualities to non-human entities.
  10. An example of anthropomorphism is: 'My dog is wagging his tail and running around me; therefore, he knows I love him.'
  11. An appeal to authority occurs when a claim is supported by citing an authority rather than evidence.
  12. An example of appeal to authority is believing the moon is covered in dust because a local authority figure said so.
  13. The appeal to emotion fallacy occurs when an argument relies on emotional response rather than logical reasoning.
  14. Appeal to emotion is similar to appeal to pity; while pity is natural, basing conclusions solely on pity is fallacious.
  15. The appeal to money fallacy assumes that something is better simply because it is expensive.
  16. An example of appeal to money is: 'He is rich, so he should be the president of our parents-teachers association.'
  17. The fallacy of avoiding the issue occurs when a speaker goes off-topic instead of addressing the main argument.
  18. The bandwagon fallacy occurs when a claim is accepted as true because many people believe it.
  19. An example of bandwagon fallacy is: 'More people are buying this car, so you should buy it too.'
  20. A double standard fallacy occurs when different standards are unfairly applied to similar cases.
  21. An example of double standard is: hiring only those who score 70%, but allowing others from a certain area to be hired with 50%.
  22. A hasty generalization occurs when a conclusion is drawn from insufficient evidence.
  23. An example of hasty generalization is: 'I met two nice people in Ghana, so all Ghanaians are nice.'
  24. Poisoning the well is a pre-emptive attempt to discredit a person or argument before it is presented.
  25. An example of poisoning the well is referring to a witness as a convicted criminal before they testify.
  26. Quibbling is a fallacy where attention is shifted to minor errors to dismiss a larger argument.
  27. An example of quibbling is rejecting a poem because of typographical errors.
  28. Rationalization occurs when people give false reasons to justify their actions.
  29. We are rationalizing when we provide reasons that are not our true motives.
  30. Inconsistency occurs when one holds contradictory beliefs at the same time.
  31. An example of inconsistency is claiming not to be racist while making contradictory statements about racial groups.
  32. Irrelevant conclusion occurs when an argument proves something different from what was intended.
  33. Scapegoating is the unjust blaming of an individual or group for a problem.
  34. Ad Hominem (circumstantial) is committed when an argument attacks a person instead of addressing their position.
  35. An example of Ad Hominem is rejecting Mr. Z as president because of personal and political disputes rather than his qualifications.
  36. Hasty conclusion (converse accident) occurs when a general rule is wrongly applied to a specific case.
  37. An example is assuming a lecturer who is absent must have gone for personal business.

GST 102: Chapter 10 — Categorical Propositions and Inferences

  1. A proposition is an assertion or sentence that has a truth value.
  2. The truth value of a proposition is whether it is true or false.
  3. The sentence 'Is Ngozi a Nigerian name?' is not a proposition because it does not state something that can be true or false; it is a question.
  4. The sentence 'Give me the GST 102 handbook' is not a proposition because it is a command and has no truth value.
  5. A proposition is either categorical or non-categorical.
  6. A proposition is a statement that asserts something without reference to conditions or alternatives.
  7. A proposition asserts something about another thing, showing that what it asserts is either true or false, for example: Nicholas is kind; Nicholas is not tall.
  8. A non-categorical proposition is one whose truth or falsity depends on conditions or alternatives.
  9. An example of a non-categorical proposition is: If Nicholas is nice, then Obi is beautiful; either Nicholas is nice or Obi is ugly.
  10. An example of a hypothetical proposition is: If Nicholas is nice, then Obi is beautiful.
  11. An example of a disjunctive proposition is: Either Nicholas is nice or Obi is ugly.
  12. The three basic parts of a categorical proposition are the subject term, predicate term, and copula.
  13. The subject term is what is being described, while the predicate term is what describes the subject.
  14. The copula is the form of the verb 'to be' that links the subject and predicate.
  15. A categorical proposition is formed by combining a subject and predicate using a copula, for example: Richard is humble; Anita is charming; women are kind-hearted.
  16. A standard form categorical proposition contains four elements: quantifier, subject term, copula, and predicate term.
  17. The most commonly used quantifiers in logic are 'all', 'some', and 'no'.
  18. The quantity of a proposition refers to how much of the subject is affirmed or denied.
  19. There are two types of quantity: universal and particular.
  20. Universal quantity refers to propositions where all members of the subject class are considered, while particular quantity refers to only some members.
  21. If the subject term is quantified by 'all' or 'no', the proposition is universal.
  22. If the subject term is quantified by 'some', the proposition is particular.
  23. Examples of universal propositions are: All trees are living things; No stones are automobiles.
  24. Examples of particular propositions are: Some men are lovely people; Some women are not kind-hearted.
  25. The quality of a proposition refers to whether it is affirmative or negative.
  26. Examples of affirmative propositions are: All trees are living things; Some men are lovely people.
  27. Examples of negative propositions are: No stones are automobiles; Some women are not friendly.
  28. The four standard form categorical propositions are: A (universal affirmative), E (universal negative), I (particular affirmative), and O (particular negative).
  29. A term is distributed if the proposition refers to every member of the class represented by that term.
  30. A term is undistributed if the proposition refers only to part of the class.
  31. Terms affected by 'no', 'all', or 'not' are distributed, while those affected by 'some' are undistributed.
  32. An example of an A-proposition is: All men are kind.
  33. An example of an E-proposition is: No men are trees.
  34. An example of an I-proposition is: Some stones are men.
  35. An example of an O-proposition is: Some mammals are not animals.
  36. Inference is the logical process of deriving a conclusion from one or more propositions.
  37. The proposition being derived is called the conclusion, while those providing support are called premises.
  38. There are two types of inference: mediate and immediate.
  39. Mediate inference draws a conclusion from two or more premises, while immediate inference draws a conclusion from a single premise.
  40. Aristotle’s Square is also called the Square of Opposition.
  41. Examples of immediate inference include conversion, obversion, and contraposition.
  42. The four relationships among standard form categorical propositions are contraries, subcontraries, contradictories, and subalternation.
  43. A and E propositions are contraries; if A is true, E is false.
  44. O and I propositions are subcontraries; both cannot be false, but both can be true.
  45. A and O propositions are contradictories, and so are E and I propositions; if one is true, the other is false.
  46. Propositions are subalternates when they share the same quality; subalternation exists between A and I, and between E and O propositions.
  47. Universal propositions are called superalterns, while particular propositions are called subalterns.

GST 111: Chapter 5 — Norms, Values, Beliefs and Practices in Nigeria

  1. Essential components of the Nigerian socio-cultural system are norms, values, and belief systems.
  2. Norms, values, and belief systems are classified under the non-material cultural configuration, also regarded as cultural universals.
  3. Norms are established social rules or guidelines that specify appropriate and inappropriate behaviour in a particular culture.
  4. Norms are rules or regulations that guide society's shared standards of behaviour.
  5. According to Otite and Ogionwo (2006), norms involve the performance of prescribed behaviour in a given situation and the imposition of sanctions for the omission of such behaviour.
  6. Values refer to beliefs, standards, and ideals about what is desirable or good behaviour and what is undesirable or bad behaviour in a particular culture.
  7. Values are the most general component of social action.
  8. Moral or ethical values necessary for societal integration and development include honesty, integrity, truth, obedience, loyalty, kindness, purity, and chastity.
  9. The National Orientation Agency identified seven core values in Nigeria: Discipline, Integrity, Dignity of Labour, Social Justice, Religious Tolerance, Self-Reliance, and Patriotism.
  10. Prescriptive norms specify appropriate and acceptable behaviour in a particular culture.
  11. Proscriptive norms specify behaviour that is inappropriate and unacceptable.
  12. Informal norms are neither written nor codified.
  13. Formal norms are written down or codified.
  14. Formalized norms are referred to as laws.
  15. Folkways are socially defined rules based on customary ways of feeling, thinking, and behaving that lack moral significance.
  16. Mores are norms held strongly with crucial moral and ethical connotations.
  17. Examples of mores include theft and having sexual intercourse with another man's wife without the husband's approval.
  18. A taboo is a variant of mores whose violation is considered extremely disgusting, repugnant, and abominable, such as primary incest.
  19. The establishment of norms in Nigerian society produces two behavioural patterns: conformity and deviance.
  20. The act of complying with established norms is called conformity, while the person who complies is a conformist.
  21. Reasons for complying with norms include fear of punishment, personal conviction, and group identification.
  22. The violation or departure from norms is called deviance, while the individual is known as a deviant.
  23. A deviant is regarded as a sinner when religious norms are violated, such as fornication and abortion.
  24. A deviant is regarded as a criminal when formal laws are violated, such as stealing, kidnapping, and murder.
  25. Negative deviance is behaviour that falls short of society's normative expectations.
  26. The causes of negative deviance include rejection of norms, lack of awareness, and misinterpretation of norms.
  27. Positive deviance results from over-conformity to norms, such as religious fanaticism.
  28. Positive deviance can create imbalance, extreme perfectionism, and disruption within society.
  29. Edwin Lemert identified primary, secondary, and tertiary deviance.
  30. Primary deviance has little effect on an individual's self-concept and provokes only slight reactions from others.
  31. An example of primary deviance is a girl engaging in prostitution for the first time; repeated involvement develops into secondary deviance.
  32. Secondary deviance marks the beginning of a deviant career.
  33. Social control is the process by which society promotes conformity and discourages the violation of norms.
  34. The two types of social control are internal social control and external social control.
  35. Internal social control means avoiding wrong behaviour because it is wrong and doing right because it is right.
  36. Social sanctions are rewards or punishments that encourage conformity.

  44. Totemism is the belief that animals, plants, or geographical features possess spiritual forces.

  46. Theism is divided into polytheism and monotheism.

  13. Rivers found in the Sokoto Plains include the Sokoto, Rima, and Zamfara Rivers, which drain the plain during the wet season.

GST 114: Chapter 2 — The French Alphabets and their Pronunciation

  1. An alphabet is a set of letters or other characters used to write one or more languages, usually arranged in a customary order.
  2. The French alphabet is made up of 26 letters.
  3. In the letters 'Aa,' the uppercase letter is called 'lettre majuscule' while the lowercase letter is called 'lettre minuscule.'
  4. In French, some letters are vowels ('les voyelles') while the others are consonants ('les consonnes').
  5. There are 36 sounds (phonemes) in French, consisting of 16 vowels, 17 consonants, and 3 semi-consonants.
  6. Most consonants at the end of French words are silent. Examples include: état, poulet, mot, départ, sujet, lit, petit, Paris, français, propos, and bras.
  7. In French, final letters such as c, r, f, l, and b are usually pronounced in words such as sac, avec, parc, pur, soif, neuf, table, and souffle.
  8. The letters b, d, f, g, k, n, s, t, v, and z are generally pronounced similarly to their English counterparts. Examples include: bœuf, dame, garçon, klaxon, nez, soie, and voiture.
  9. French speakers are generally more formal when addressing one another than Nigerians. Among friends, the informal greeting 'Salut' is commonly used.
  10. The verb 'Aller' means 'to go.'

GST 114: Chapter 3 — La Famille

  1. The statement 'A family is one social unit which comprises a father, mother, and children living together under the same roof' in French is: 'La famille est l'ensemble des personnes apparentées vivant sous le même toit.'

  5. For pleasantries in French: 'Bonne chance' means 'Good luck,' 'Bon voyage' means 'Safe journey,' and 'Bon appétit' means 'Have a nice meal.'
  6. When the letter 's' occurs between two vowels, it is pronounced as /z/. For example, 'les enfants' is pronounced 'lez enfants.'

GST 114: Chapter 4 — Direction

  1. Some French words indicating direction include: 'À' meaning 'to/at,' 'dans' meaning 'in,' 'de' meaning 'from/of,' 'avec' meaning 'with,' 'sans' meaning 'without,' and 'devant' meaning 'in front of.'
  2. The best way to determine the meaning of a French word is by understanding it within the context of a sentence and its relationship with other words.
  3. The verb 'Aller' is an irregular verb. Some of its conjugations are: 'Je vais' (I go), 'Tu vas' (You go), 'Il va' (He goes), and 'Nous allons' (We go).

GST 114: Chapter 7 — Le Pluriel en Francais/Plurality in French

  1. The plural of most French nouns is formed by adding 's' to the singular form. For example: 'La maison' becomes 'Les maisons' and 'Le patron' becomes 'Les patrons.'
  2. In French, nouns ending in 's', 'x', or 'z' do not change in the plural. For example: 'Le pois' becomes 'Les pois' (pea/peas), and 'La croix' becomes 'Les croix' (cross/crosses).
  3. In French, many nouns ending in '-al' change to '-aux' in the plural. For example: 'Le canal' becomes 'Les canaux' (canal/canals), and 'Le journal' becomes 'Les journaux' (newspaper/newspapers).
  4. In French, nouns ending in '-au', '-eau', or '-eu' usually take an 'x' in the plural. For example: 'Le noyau' becomes 'Les noyaux' (stone/stones), 'Le manteau' becomes 'Les manteaux' (coat/coats), and 'Le jeu' becomes 'Les jeux' (game/games).
  5. In French, many nouns ending in '-ou' take an 'x' in the plural. For example: 'Le bijou' becomes 'Les bijoux' (jewel/jewels), 'Le caillou' becomes 'Les cailloux' (pebble/pebbles), and 'Le chou' becomes 'Les choux' (cabbage/cabbages).
  6. Demonstrative adjectives are used to indicate or point out a person, animal, or object. In French, the singular forms are 'ce', 'cet', and 'cette', while the plural form is 'ces'. For example: 'Ce jardin' becomes 'Ces jardins' in the plural.
